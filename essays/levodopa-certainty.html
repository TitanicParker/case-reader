Essay 2

If Levodopa Was Withheld, Certainty Had to Be Withheld Too

On therapeutic caution versus diagnostic discrimination.

Thesis They did not have to give levodopa. But if they did not give levodopa, they had to leave the diagnosis open.

The levodopa decision is the point at which the case becomes easiest to misunderstand and hardest to excuse.

It is easy to misunderstand because a defence can make the decision look clinically respectable. The patient was young. Early levodopa exposure in young Parkinsonian patients can carry long-term complications. Dyskinesias, impulse-control difficulties, and treatment sequencing are real concerns. A cautious clinician may decide not to rush toward the strongest dopaminergic therapy. On that narrow therapeutic axis, the decision can be argued. It may even look prudent.

But this case does not turn on the narrow therapeutic axis.

It turns on the diagnostic consequence of that therapeutic decision.

In this presentation, levodopa was not merely medication. It was information. It was not merely a treatment that might relieve symptoms; it was a discriminator that could clarify what kind of disorder was present, how the patient’s rigidity and gait related to dopaminergic responsiveness, and whether the severe foot-centred syndrome belonged inside the movement disorder rather than outside it. To withhold levodopa was therefore not only to delay therapy. It was to delay knowledge.

That distinction is the heart of the case.

The argument is not that levodopa absolutely had to be started immediately in July 2017. That is too blunt and too vulnerable. The stronger argument is more precise: if levodopa was not started, diagnostic certainty had to be withheld too. The institution could choose therapeutic caution, but it could not safely combine therapeutic caution with administrative finality.

The record appears to do exactly that. It defers levodopa, but it does not defer certainty. It withholds the discriminator, but it allows the diagnosis to harden. It preserves caution around medication, but not around finality. That is the sequencing error.

A safe discharge could have said: “This is suspected young-onset Parkinsonism. Dopaminergic therapy is being deferred for long-term management reasons. Because response to levodopa may carry diagnostic significance, the diagnosis remains provisional. The foot-centred symptoms and dystonic gait require further review, ideally by a movement-disorder specialist.” That would have preserved the truth of the situation. It would have allowed caution without pretending the diagnostic sequence had completed.

Instead, the record moves toward Parkinson’s Disease as an administrative fact.

That is the problem.

The phrase “we may regret that we did not start this earlier” matters because it exposes the clinician’s own sense of risk. This was not a decision presented as consequence-free. It was not a decision framed as obviously irrelevant to the patient’s future. It was a decision made under caution, with an admitted possibility of future regret.

Read only as treatment, the sentence means: one day the patient may suffer because medication was delayed. But in this particular case, the sentence has a second meaning. It means: one day we may regret not applying the intervention that could have clarified the case before the diagnosis hardened.

That is why the sentence is so powerful. It is not merely compassionate. It is diagnostic foreboding.

The patient’s presentation made the foreboding sharper. This was not a typical older patient with classic asymmetric tremor-predominant Parkinson’s disease. This was a young man with a long history, severe foot-centred pain, abnormal gait, a movement resembling foot drop but explicitly not foot drop, dystonic suggestion, brisk reflexes, minimal tremor, preserved cognition, and possible genetic disease. In that kind of case, response to dopaminergic therapy is not a peripheral curiosity. It has potential explanatory force.

If the feet improved

That would matter to causation, classification, and whether the foot-centred syndrome belonged inside the movement-disorder frame.

If gait or rigidity changed

That would matter to dopaminergic responsiveness and the safety of treating the diagnosis as settled.

If pain responded, that would matter. The response could help decide whether the feet were dermatological, peripheral, central, dystonic, Parkinsonian, genetic, or some overlapping combination. It could help prevent the most dangerous error in the case: exporting the feet to podiatry while certifying Parkinson’s Disease elsewhere.

So when levodopa was withheld, the diagnostic inquiry remained open. That is not hindsight. That follows from the logic of the decision at the time. The team did not apply a material discriminator. Therefore the diagnosis had not been discriminated by that route. That fact had to be preserved.

It was not.

This is where therapeutic caution becomes administrative danger. A clinician may say, “I am delaying levodopa because I want to protect this young patient’s long-term outcome.” That may be reasonable. But the same clinician cannot then allow the record to speak as if the condition has been sufficiently settled by other means, unless the unresolved implications of non-testing are made explicit. The more justified the caution, the more necessary the provisionality.

The two should rise together.

If caution is high, certainty should be low.

In this case, caution was high and certainty was high. That is the contradiction.

The senior-advice dimension intensifies the issue. If the treating neurologist was operating under advice not to give “the good stuff,” then the deferral was not merely his own prescription preference. It was an accepted strategy shaped by authority. That makes the decision more institutional, not less. The neurologist may have felt the tension. He may have understood the therapeutic rationale. He may also have understood the risk. But he was not in a position to easily challenge the policy-shaped instruction.

That explains the strange emotional temperature of the record. It is careful, qualified, foreboding. It does not sound like triumphant diagnostic certainty. It sounds like a clinician trying to maintain a line that has been set, while documenting enough discomfort to show he recognised danger. That is not exculpatory. It is revealing.

A policy-shaped decision to withhold the discriminator creates an institutional obligation to avoid finality. If the senior strategy was “do not start levodopa yet,” then the corresponding governance strategy should have been “do not finalise yet.” The institution cannot have it both ways. It cannot protect the future by withholding levodopa while endangering the future by hardening an undifferentiated diagnosis.

This is the cleanest statement of the case:

They did not have to give levodopa. But if they did not give levodopa, they had to leave the diagnosis open.

That line matters because it avoids overreach. It does not ask a court, expert, or regulator to say that immediate levodopa was mandatory. It asks them to say something much harder to deny: diagnostic certainty was not mandatory, and should not have been exercised while a key discriminator was deliberately unused.

The Long Term Illness certification makes this especially serious. A provisional diagnosis inside a clinician’s reasoning is one thing. A certified diagnosis used for statutory purposes is another. Once Parkinson’s Disease is certified, it leaves the zone of tentative thought and enters the zone of administrative reliance. That reliance will shape future prescribing, GP understanding, patient identity, record review, and complaint response.

So the question becomes sharper still: was it appropriate to certify Parkinson’s Disease while the principal dopaminergic discriminator had been withheld for strategic reasons and while the initiating foot-centred problem remained unresolved?

That is not a medical curiosity. It is a governance question.

It is also a record-accuracy question. The record should reflect not only what was suspected but what had not been tested. A truthful record of the July 2017 state would have made clear that the diagnosis was provisional, that levodopa response had not yet been assessed, that the foot problem remained unexplained, and that specialist movement-disorder review may be required. Such a record would have protected the patient, the GP, future clinicians, and the institution itself.

Instead, the record gave future readers a settled line and buried the incompleteness in the detail.

That is why the levodopa issue is so damaging. It is not about one tablet. It is about the relationship between treatment, knowledge, and finality.

A system can delay treatment.

A system can preserve uncertainty.

A system can seek specialist review.

A system can certify cautiously if it clearly qualifies what is and is not known.

What it cannot safely do is delay the discriminator, omit the diagnostic consequence of that delay, and proceed as though the discriminated diagnosis has been earned.

That is the breach.

The final point is ethical as well as clinical. The patient was cognitively preserved. The record recognised that he could understand nuance. If levodopa was being deferred in a way that affected not only treatment but diagnostic knowledge, that should have been explained. The patient should have been told, plainly, that the medication being delayed might also clarify the condition, and that delaying it meant accepting continuing uncertainty. Without that explanation, the patient could only understand the decision as therapeutic caution. He could not understand it as diagnostic non-testing.

That manufactured ignorance matters.

It meant that when the patient later protested that the feet had not been addressed, he did so without the technical architecture that would have made the protest impossible to neutralise. He knew the body had not been answered. He did not know that a key route to answering it had been deliberately withheld.

This is why levodopa sits at the centre.

It was the withheld drug, the withheld test, the withheld explanation, and the withheld limit on certainty.

The proper rule is simple:

If levodopa was withheld, certainty had to be withheld too.

The record did the opposite.
