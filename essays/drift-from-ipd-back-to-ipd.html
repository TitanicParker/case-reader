Essay 11

The Drift from iPD Back to iPD

On recurring destabilisation, explanatory capture, and the return of every contradiction to idiopathic Parkinson’s disease.

Thesis Not that iPD was never possible. That iPD became the place where every contradiction was sent to disappear.

Evidence note

This essay is an interpretive companion. It relies on the longitudinal neurology record, the iPD framing, genetic possibility, medication-response evidence, foot pain, dystonia, and later rebuttal correspondence.

There is a strange circularity in the neurology record. It begins with a confident drift into idiopathic Parkinson’s disease, wanders repeatedly through facts that should destabilise that label, and then returns to idiopathic Parkinson’s disease as if the journey itself had confirmed the starting point. That is the drift from iPD back to iPD. Not a straight line. Not a clean diagnosis. A loop. A record that moves away from certainty often enough to show that certainty was not secure, but then always comes home to the same administrative harbour.

The significance of this is not that iPD was an absurd starting point. It was not. A young man presents with Parkinsonism, bradykinesia, rigidity, gait disturbance, abnormal DAT scan, and response to dopaminergic therapies. A Parkinsonian frame was plainly available. The issue is not whether iPD could be suspected. The issue is that the record repeatedly encountered reasons to treat iPD as incomplete, provisional, or possibly wrong in kind, yet each encounter was metabolised back into iPD rather than allowed to govern a new diagnostic sequence.

That is what makes the record so revealing. It is not static. It does not simply say “iPD” once and stop thinking. It keeps thinking. It keeps recording anomalies. It keeps registering the feet. It keeps acknowledging atypicality. It keeps entertaining genetic disease. It keeps observing dystonic or choreiform phenomena. It keeps recognising levodopa sensitivity. It keeps treating pain as oddly responsive to Parkinson’s medication while insisting pain is not directly Parkinson’s. And yet the explanatory centre always returns to idiopathic Parkinson’s disease.

The drift begins in the original diagnostic language. The early record frames the case as Parkinsonism, then young-onset Parkinson’s, then likely idiopathic Parkinson’s “albeit atypical,” while also recognising features that strain the ordinary iPD frame: very young onset, possible symptoms for thirteen years, lack of prominent tremor, symmetry, and possible genetic Parkinsonism. The 2017 clinical indication for the DAT scan itself calls the case “very young,” “bilateral,” “hard to say which side is worse,” “grossly bradykinetic,” “in many respects atypical,” and notes that the walk is “almost dystonically unusual,” before ending with the revealing phrase: “We suspect an Autosomal recessive ‘Idiopathic’, Parkinsons.”

That phrase is almost the whole case in miniature: “autosomal recessive” and “idiopathic” are being held together in a way that should create tension.

Autosomal recessive suggests a genetic cause; idiopathic means no known cause. The phrase reveals a mind trying to keep the inherited iPD category while admitting that the presentation may belong somewhere else. It is a hybrid phrase, an unstable compromise. It says: this looks like it may be genetic, but we are still going to route it through the idiopathic frame.

From there, the record repeatedly has opportunities to drift away from iPD. The first is genetics. In the original discharge correspondence, PRKN/PARK2 is not absent. It is actively considered. The reasoning is not blind to it. The record notes that very young age of onset, akinetic-rigid presentation, lack of dominant family history, marked symmetry, and excellent cognition raise the question of PRKN/PARK2. It also acknowledges rarer monogenic explanations. But having brought genetics into the room, the record deprives it of operational force. Testing may not change management. Testing may be negative. Perhaps whole genome sequencing would be better later. There is no urgent reclassification, no firm movement-disorder referral, no suspension of iPD finality. Genetics becomes recognised but postponed.

That is the first drift: from iPD toward genetic Parkinsonism, then back to iPD.

The second drift is levodopa. In 2017, the record says ordinary treatment would use levodopa or dopamine agonists, but in “these types of situations” the team is “extremely cautious” and will leave off as long as possible. The record then says Liam understands why levodopa or dopamine agonists are being delayed, and that they may later have to be introduced. Then comes the foreboding sentence: “Indeed in time to come we may regret that we did not start this earlier.”

That sentence does not sit comfortably in a purely iPD-maintenance frame. If the diagnosis is secure and the decision is simply therapeutic caution, regret means delayed symptom relief. But in a young, atypical, foot-centred case, levodopa also carries diagnostic significance. A response could have helped clarify the nature of the disorder, the role of dystonia, and whether the foot pain belonged inside the movement disorder. Deferring levodopa therefore deferred discrimination. The record acknowledges possible regret but does not let that possible regret alter finality. The drift is again visible: from iPD toward incompletely discriminated Parkinsonism, then back to iPD.

The third drift is the feet. The feet are never safely gone. They return across the years like an unresolved clause in the body. In April 2018, after withdrawal of procyclidine, the record notes that the “first thing” to change rapidly was the return “on the foot, of the outside left corn,” and that symptoms worsened off procyclidine. That is a major central-modulation clue. The foot phenomenon changes with a centrally acting Parkinson’s medication. The record also notes that missing procyclidine makes him feel tighter in the left bottom/piriformis, and that his movement while seated is “more than would be customarily expected in the hypokinetic disorder that is iPD.”

This should have pulled the case away from simple iPD. The foot phenomenon is not merely a corn. It is a recurring, medication-sensitive, focal motor-pain signal. The record has the clue. But it does not allow the clue to reorganise the case. Procyclidine remains management. The feet remain symptoms. iPD remains the frame.

The drift repeats in 2020. The record notes a “bad corn on the outside of the LEFT foot,” a “throw” in the left lower limb, “exquisite sensitivity of the feet,” excessive movement, and urge to move the feet. It says the foot throw looks a little like foot drop but is “actually parkinsonian” because power is normal. That is another crucial moment. If power is normal, then the apparent foot drop is not peripheral weakness. If it is “actually parkinsonian,” then the feet are inside the central movement disorder. But instead of making foot dystonia or genetic dystonic Parkinsonism governing, the record folds it back into iPD. The sign is acknowledged as central but absorbed into the existing label.

That is the drift again: from iPD toward dystonic foot-centred Parkinsonism, then back to iPD.

The fourth drift appears in the introduction of Sinemet in 2021. Sinemet begins as a substitution when Kemadrin is removed because of ocular pressure concerns. The record acknowledges that the Sinemet dose escalates quickly and that the effective dose may be higher because of Comtess. It states clearly that in young patients the global principle is to spare Sinemet for as long as possible and keep dose minimal. Again, the record is careful. But the carefulness remains therapeutic. The diagnostic implication is not allowed to govern. Sinemet response is not treated as a delayed discriminator that should force a retrospective re-reading of the 2017 uncertainty. It becomes part of iPD management.

The fifth drift is the 2023 crisis. This is one of the clearest episodes. The patient reports severe bilateral burning foot and lower-leg pain, extreme at times, causing a “death wish,” with Lyrica increased up to 600mg/day and “only just now controlling the symptoms.” The GP’s urgent referral explicitly says he is worried and asks for review “before we get into trouble here.” The nurse’s message the same day records ongoing neuropathic pain, debilitating pain, no quality of life, worsening over months, and the patient’s view that it is not Parkinson-related.

This should have been a major re-entry point into the foot problem. Severe foot pain, near-crisis psychological consequence, high-dose pregabalin, and a patient insisting on a separate problem: this is not background noise. It is the original signal returning with escalation. Yet the subsequent July 2023 letter again drifts back to Parkinson’s logic. It acknowledges that the “major problem” is the foot pain, records cramping in the left lower limb raising the question of Parkinsonian dystonia, likely OFF dystonia, notes that peripheral neuropathy does not come on literally overnight, acknowledges the patient’s “very reasonable and justifiable concerns” that much relates to the feet as a separate problem, and even says there is “misalignment” between the clinician’s belief that everything has to be explainable by Parkinson’s and the patient’s concern about the feet.

That passage is extraordinary. The record itself names the disagreement: the clinician has a firm belief that everything has to be explainable by Parkinson’s; the patient has reasonable and justifiable concerns that much relates to the feet separately.

This is the drift exposed openly. The clinician sees the possibility that the frame is too totalising. He sees the foot question. He sees OFF dystonia. He sees that peripheral neuropathy does not fit sudden onset. He sees that Lyrica has changed the pain. He sees that Stalevo facilitates function. And still the gravitational pull is iPD.

The July 2023 letter even says that Liam describes taking Stalevo for “Pep in the Step” and “for pain,” and that both can be understood “in the context of how levodopa works in iPD.” Pain improvement is therefore taken back into iPD as a non-motor fluctuation or OFF-state magnification. The pain response to levodopa does not destabilise iPD; it is used to reinforce iPD.

That is the drift in its purest form. The foot pain appears to challenge the frame. The medication response appears to challenge the frame. The record acknowledges the challenge. Then the challenge is translated into the language of iPD and returned to the frame.

By 2024 and 2025, the loop becomes even tighter. The 2025 neurology note states: “The one thing that is on Liam’s mind is foot pain.” It records “murder in the feet,” the first 150 footsteps as “torture,” “I won’t live with it,” and the fact that once Parkinson’s medications hit his system he has relief in the feet. But the impression then says: “Quite simply, pain is not really a direct part of Parkinson’s at all,” while also acknowledging that painful dystonia can occur and that increasing Sinemet would probably give further relief, though at the cost of dyskinesias.

That is the contradiction at the end of the loop. Pain is not Parkinson’s; Sinemet helps the pain; painful dystonia can occur; more Sinemet would likely help further; but the pain remains framed as not direct Parkinson’s phenomenology and Lyrica is suggested as the safer route because increasing Parkinson’s medication risks dyskinesia. The clinical management concern may be reasonable. But diagnostically, the record is again refusing to let the response govern. It says the dopaminergic system affects the foot pain, but not in a way that changes the explanatory label.

The March 2025 rebuttal makes the drift explicit as a defence. Dr Monaghan states that “whatever the underlying cause for the peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies,” it remains “our belief and understanding” that Liam has iPD. He says Parkinson’s magnifies symptoms from other causes, including pain. He accepts that Sinemet helps the experience of pain, but explains it as Parkinson’s effect on pain experience “of whatever cause,” and says the reluctance to increase Sinemet was due to dyskinesia and other risks.

This is iPD as absorptive frame. It can absorb pain by saying Parkinson’s magnifies it. It can absorb Sinemet response by saying Sinemet reduces pain experience without proving pain is caused by Parkinson’s. It can absorb alcohol by saying neuropathy remains possible but not exclusive. It can absorb Lyrica by saying it was used to spare Sinemet. It can absorb Sinemet CR relief by saying this is consistent with known Parkinson’s management. Everything returns to iPD.

The rebuttal even quotes the original 2017 passage about delaying levodopa and then summarises the position: “we have known that Mr Moloney has Parkinson’s; we have known it would be Sinemet or other dopamine agonist responsive; we have tried to spare commencing those for some time as is common practice.” That sentence is astonishing because it retrospectively converts the unresolved 2017 diagnostic question into a known fact. “We have known” replaces “we suspected.” The drift is complete. The case begins with atypical Parkinsonism and possible genetic disease, moves through years of foot-centred contradiction, and returns in 2025 as: we have known he has iPD.

But the record did not simply know. It repeatedly negotiated.

That is the point.

The drift from iPD back to iPD is not a story of stable certainty. It is a story of recurring destabilisation followed by reabsorption. Genetic possibility is reabsorbed. Dystonia is reabsorbed. Foot pain is reabsorbed. Levodopa response is reabsorbed. Procyclidine response is reabsorbed. Patient protest is reabsorbed or omitted. Neuropathic labels are reabsorbed. Even severe pain with death-wish escalation is reabsorbed.

This is not just diagnostic anchoring in the loose sense. It is explanatory capture.

Once iPD becomes the governing frame, all later facts are required to become either expressions of iPD, complications of iPD, magnifications by iPD, unrelated peripheral symptoms coexisting with iPD, or psychological/pain-experience phenomena occurring under iPD. No fact is allowed to become the fact that changes the frame.

That is why “back to iPD” is so important. The record wanders, but it does not migrate. It explores alternatives but does not relocate. It tests the elasticity of iPD rather than the adequacy of iPD. The question becomes not “what diagnosis best explains the record?” but “how can this new fact be made consistent with iPD?”

And because iPD is broad enough to absorb many things, the frame survives. Parkinson’s can magnify pain. OFF states can worsen pain. Levodopa can improve pain experience. Dystonia can be painful. Neuropathy can coexist. Alcohol can contribute. Lyrica can help neuropathic symptoms. Sinemet can help movement and thereby pain. Each statement can be individually plausible. The danger lies in the total effect: every contradictory sign becomes non-contradictory once translated into the iPD dialect.

That is how the frame protects itself.

A fair expert does not need to say that iPD was impossible. The expert only needs to see that iPD became unfalsifiable in practice. If levodopa response supports iPD, but pain relief on levodopa does not challenge the pain label; if dystonic foot signs are “parkinsonian” but do not reopen the genetic/dystonia frame; if PRKN is suspected but testing is delayed because management may not change; if non-specialist status is recorded but finality proceeds; if the patient’s reasonable concern about the feet is acknowledged but subordinated to the clinician’s belief that everything is explainable by Parkinson’s, then the diagnostic process is no longer truly open.

It has become circular.

The conclusion explains the evidence, and the evidence is selected or translated to preserve the conclusion.

That is the drift from iPD back to iPD.

It is not one decision. It is a habit of record. A way of making every road return to the same place. A young, atypical, foot-centred, dystonic, genetically plausible, levodopa-sensitive case repeatedly tries to leave the idiopathic category. The record repeatedly follows it partway. Then, at the critical moment, it turns back.

And each return makes the next return easier. By 2025, the system can say: we have known he has iPD. But the documents show something subtler and more damaging. They show that the system repeatedly encountered the limits of that knowledge and chose not to let those limits govern.

That is the case.

Not that iPD was never possible.

That iPD became the place where every contradiction was sent to disappear.
