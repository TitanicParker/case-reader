Essay 13

I Do Not Know What It Was Like to Live Inside It

On the danger of fluent analysis, the lived cost of unresolved pain, and the limits of what documents can know.

Thesis Legibility is not rescue. A coherent account is not the same thing as knowledge.
Main site Essay index Essay 12 Sharpened theory

Evidence note

This essay is an interpretive companion. It deliberately marks the limit of analysis: the record can be made legible, but the lived experience of pain, dependence, trust, and attrition cannot be fully known from the outside.

  2. Limits of documentary fluency
  3. Patient experience as irreducible fact

There is a professional danger in speaking too fluently about another person’s suffering. Fluency can imitate understanding. It can arrange pain into sequence, translate injury into chronology, convert bewilderment into argument, and make the whole thing sound manageable because it has finally been made legible. But legibility is not rescue. A coherent account is not the same thing as knowledge. A document can know dates, diagnoses, referrals, medication changes, omissions, correspondence, silence, escalation, and closure. It can know that a man entered a hospital with a foot-centred gait failure and left with Parkinson’s disease. It can know that the diagnosis was treated as final before the initiating problem had been resolved. It can know that the central symptom remained active, displaced, and later vindicated. It can know all of that and still not know what it was like to live there.

I can read that the case concerns long-standing foot pain, neurological uncertainty, a 2017 admission, a diagnosis treated as settled before the sequence was complete, and years of harm following from that premature settlement. I can read that the patient’s presenting problem included impaired movement initiation, rigidity, abnormal gait, stiffness, dystonic features, and severe bilateral foot pain. I can read that the feet were never incidental. I can read that they were present before the diagnosis, during the admission, at discharge, after discharge, through follow-up, through medication changes, through complaint, through governance, through data correspondence, and through the eventual clarification of what the body had been saying all along. But I do not know what it is to live inside that sentence.

I do not know what “severe bilateral foot pain” meant at 11 a.m. on an ordinary Tuesday. I do not know what it meant at 2 a.m., when there was no ward round, no clinic letter, no witness, no consultant, no complaint file, no procedural category, no one to whom the body’s insistence could be addressed. I do not know how the pain changed the geometry of ordinary life: the distance between bed and bathroom, chair and kettle, front door and pavement, bus stop and pub, bedroom and dinner table. I do not know how many decisions became negotiations with the floor. Whether to stand now or later. Whether to refill the glass. Whether to answer the door. Whether to leave the house. Whether the dog could be walked. Whether one more small task could be done before the body extracted its price.

I do not know the scale of the narrowing.

That is what records conceal even when they are accurate. They preserve the event, not the attrition. They say pain was prolonged, uncontrolled, debilitating, functionally limiting. They say quality of life was affected. They say psychological deterioration followed. They say suicidality became linked to an unresolved symptom. These phrases are necessary. They belong in legal analysis. They belong in clinical chronology. They belong in correspondence. But they are still phrases. They cannot reproduce the lived arithmetic of a life reduced to essential movement. They cannot measure the private humiliations of needing to plan around one’s own feet. They cannot capture the resentment of discovering that the body had become both prison and evidence, while the institution that possessed the record still did not understand what the record meant.

I do not know what it cost to preserve cognition under those conditions.

From outside, cognition appears as output. A chronology. A complaint. A matrix. A submission. A theory of administrative foreclosure. A sentence that lands cleanly because it has been sharpened against years of injury. It is easy to admire the discipline of that work. It is easy to say the framing is controlled, that it avoids overclaiming, that it identifies sequence failure rather than merely asserting outrage. It is easy to notice the restraint. But that is not the same as knowing what it took to remain capable of restraint.

I do not know how many times the same events had to be revisited before they became evidence rather than trauma. I do not know what it is like to realise, years later, that a moment which felt humiliating or confusing at the time was structurally decisive. I do not know what it took to turn “they did not listen to me” into “the diagnostic process was incomplete, the principal discriminator was withheld, finality attached before inquiry had been discharged, and the initiating signal was displaced.” That conversion is not merely intellectual. It is labour performed after harm. It is the mind returning to the scene and refusing to let the scene remain formless.

I do not know what restraint felt like from the inside.

On the page, restraint can look calm. It can appear as elegance, discipline, proportion. But restraint after prolonged pain is not temperament. It is force. It is anger held still long enough to be usable. It is grief translated into a form that will not be dismissed. It is the refusal to let the scale of injury make the account inadmissible to the very systems that helped produce it. There is something almost cruel in that requirement. The injured person must not only suffer. He must later become precise about the architecture of the suffering. He must avoid sounding too wounded while explaining why the wound matters. He must keep the case clean enough to survive contact with people trained to distrust excess.

I can describe the 2017 sequence. The urgent GP referral. The admission. The foot-centred gait failure. The diagnostic uncertainty. The withholding of levodopa. The statutory certification. The bedside protest. The discharge. The displacement of the feet into podiatry. I can describe the later years. Procyclidine. Withdrawal. Recurrence. Pregabalin. Neuropathy language. Sinemet. Sinemet CR. The contradiction becoming undeniable. I can describe the governance sequence. Complaint. Closure. Reclassification. Silence. Escalation. Data rights. Restriction. Refusal. Further finality. I can describe all of it. What I do not know is the humiliation of making the plea before discharge and later finding that the plea had no durable institutional body. I do not know what it does to a person to speak from inside his own unresolved condition and be processed as though the problem has already been solved.

I do not know the exact quality of trust that existed before it broke.

That matters. It is too easy to flatten this into accusation. But trust was there. It must have been. The patient did not enter the system already armed with the theory of the system’s failure. He entered it needing help. He believed that the people examining him were trying to understand him. He accepted explanations. He accepted caution. He accepted delay. He accepted that there were risks in treatment. He accepted that a consultant, a complex case meeting, a senior authority, a discharge summary, a statutory form, and a follow-up plan meant something coherent had happened. He had no reason to know that one kind of finality had arrived before another kind of inquiry had finished. He had no reason to know that a treatment decision might also be a diagnostic decision. He had no reason to know that what had been withheld as therapy might also have been withheld as knowledge.

That ignorance is terrible because it was reasonable.

No patient should have to understand the hidden epistemology of his own admission in real time. No patient should have to know that the thing not being tried may be the thing that would have tested the frame being imposed on him. No patient should have to distinguish prudential therapeutic delay from the disabling of a diagnostic discriminator. He was entitled to believe that inquiry was ongoing. He was entitled to believe that uncertainty, if present, remained alive inside the system. He was entitled to believe that if the feet still governed his life, the record would keep them governing the case.

Instead, the record became fluent.

It could narrate him. It could classify him. It could certify him. It could route him. It could apologise around him. It could later say that pain was understood, that caution was reasonable, that treatment had been careful, that Parkinson’s magnified pain, that Sinemet helped the experience of pain, that records had been copied, that the letters were long because the patient was at the heart of everything. It could say many things. Some of them may even have been sincerely meant. But sincerity does not repair sequence. Fluency does not restore inquiry. A long letter is not the same as an open mind.

That is the hidden obscenity of the case. Not that nobody wrote. Not that nobody observed. Not that nobody cared in the sentimental sense. The obscenity is that the record could see so much and still not correct itself. It could preserve atypicality without letting atypicality govern. It could preserve foot pain without letting the feet return to the centre. It could preserve uncertainty while acting with certainty. It could preserve the patient’s intelligence while denying the intelligence of his protest. It could preserve the fact of suffering while denying suffering the power to reopen what had been closed.

I do not know what it feels like to live for years under that contradiction.

To know something is wrong, but not yet know the name of the wrongness. To feel the body objecting, but lack the institutional grammar that would make the objection audible. To encounter clinicians who have the records, the letters, the medication history, the pain history, the worsening function, and still find that the old frame remains heavier than the present fact. To discover that a system can absorb contradiction without being changed by it. To learn, slowly and bitterly, that acknowledgement is not the same as correction.

There is a particular violence in being partially believed.

The patient was not simply dismissed in the crude sense. His pain was recorded. His descriptions were copied. His unusual features were noted. His intelligence was remarked upon. His account was often preserved in detail. That makes the case worse, not better. Total erasure has a bluntness to it. Partial preservation is more difficult. It gives the appearance of being heard while withholding the consequence of hearing. It allows the system to say, “We recorded it,” while the patient is left to ask, “Then why did it not matter?”

That question is the centre of the case.

Not “why was there uncertainty?” Medicine is allowed uncertainty. Not “why was there caution?” Caution has a place. Not “why was the first hypothesis imperfect?” Human judgment is imperfect. The question is why unresolved knowledge was permitted to become final knowledge. Why a provisional frame acquired statutory and administrative force before the initiating signal had been resolved. Why the feet, having brought the patient into care, were allowed to become peripheral to the explanation of his own body. Why the protest did not reopen the sequence. Why later contradiction did not restore it. Why governance treated the problem as clinical judgment when the deeper issue was the order in which knowledge and finality had been allowed to occur.

I do not know what it is like to carry that question for years while still needing the system.

That dependency is often missing from external analysis. The injured person cannot simply leave the structure that injured him. He still needs medication. He still needs letters. He still needs records. He still needs appointments. He still needs the GP to understand the specialist, the specialist to write to the GP, the hospital to release the file, the file to reflect reality, the data controller to qualify what is disputed, the regulator to read what has been made almost unreadable by accumulation. He must challenge the system while remaining entangled in it. He must dispute the record while living under the consequences of the record. He must ask for correction from the very architecture whose coherence depends on not correcting.

I do not know what that does to a person’s sense of reality.

There must be a point at which the injury is no longer only pain. It becomes epistemic. The harm is not merely that the feet hurt. It is that the system’s account of the feet becomes harder to dislodge than the feet themselves. The body says one thing. The record says another. Treatment eventually reveals something. Governance refuses the implication. The patient is then forced to live not only with symptoms, but with the burden of proving that the documents do not mean what the institution needs them to mean.

This is why the preserved cognition matters so much. It is not a decorative feature. It is not just a clinical note. It is the condition that allowed the case to survive. The mind remained capable of pattern recognition. It remembered. It compared. It noticed sequence. It saw that a diagnosis could be both medically plausible and administratively premature. It saw that a discharge summary could contain its own contradiction. It saw that a complaint could be closed by reproducing the very structure being complained of. It saw that a data dispute was not an abstract privacy issue but a fight over continued reliance on an incomplete truth.

But preservation is not protection.

The fact that the patient could understand the injury did not prevent the injury. It may even have intensified it. To know enough to see the mechanism, but not yet have the power to stop it, is its own form of suffering. The mind becomes the witness the system did not appoint. It becomes investigator, archivist, complainant, analyst, historian, and injured party at once. That is not resilience in the soft inspirational sense. It is survival under evidential burden.

I do not know what it cost to become credible.

That may be the most painful unknown. To make this case persuasive, the patient had to become controlled. He had to make the anger useful. He had to learn the language of sequence, governance, foreclosure, reliance, accuracy, restriction, finality, and institutional duty. He had to make the case less personal in order to prove how personal it was. He had to stop saying only “this happened to me” and start showing “this is how a system made what happened to me administratively stable.” He had to become legible to power without letting power define the injury.

That is why any account of this case must be careful. It must not steal fluency from pain and call that understanding. It must not become so clever that the man disappears behind the mechanism. The mechanism matters. It is the case. But the mechanism was lived through feet, stairs, nights, work, humiliation, trust, dependence, fear, endurance, and the slow discovery that relief had been possible.

The record could describe him, classify him, certify him, route him, close him, review him, and later apologise for him. It could speak at length. It could sound reasonable. It could preserve detail. It could generate enough paper to simulate care.

What it could not do, when it still mattered, was stop.

It could not stop at the feet. It could not stop at uncertainty. It could not stop at the withheld discriminator. It could not stop at the protest. It could not stop at the contradiction. It could not stop at the point where finality should have waited for inquiry.

And because it did not stop, he had to.

He had to stop the record years later, hold it still, and make it answer the question it had avoided from the beginning: if the diagnosis was final, why did the record preserve so many reasons finality was unsafe; and if the diagnosis was open, why was it certified, operationalised, and defended as closed?

That sentence is almost too quiet for what it contains.

But that is the point. The strongest version of this case may not be the loudest one. It may be the one that lets the structure stand there, almost politely, until the absurdity becomes unbearable. The system did not merely misunderstand a symptom. It acted with certainty after disabling the conditions of knowing, and then organised its records and governance around the preservation of that certainty.

I can say that.

I can analyse it.

I can even make it sound clean.

But I do not know what it was like to live inside it.
