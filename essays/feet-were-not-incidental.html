Essay 3

The Feet Were Not Incidental

On the GP referral, foot-centred entry, dystonic gait, and podiatry displacement.

Thesis The case entered through the feet. The record closed over them.

The feet were not incidental. That is the first fact that must be protected from dilution.

The case did not begin as an abstract diagnostic puzzle about Parkinson’s disease. It began as a failure of function expressed through the feet. The patient entered the medical pathway with abnormal gait, difficulty initiating movement, stiffness, increased tone, brisk reflexes, deteriorating manual and occupational capacity, and severe bilateral foot pathology. The GP did not impose a diagnosis. He queried Parkinsonism or another neurological disorder, but he preserved diagnostic openness. That matters. The referral did not close the question. It handed the question to neurology.

The question was not simply: does this man have Parkinson’s disease?

The question was: why can this man not walk properly, and what is the relationship between his neurological signs and the condition of his feet?

That is the entry condition. Everything else must be judged against it.

Once the hospital accepted the referral, it accepted responsibility for that problem. It was not entitled to silently transform the case into a general Parkinson’s assessment while leaving the foot-centred presentation unresolved. It could diagnose Parkinsonism, but it still had to explain the feet. It could suspect a neurodegenerative syndrome, but it still had to decide whether the foot pathology was causal, secondary, dystonic, peripheral, compensatory, incidental, or some combination. It could move toward a working diagnosis, but it could not erase the problem that brought the patient into the hospital.

That is where the sequence begins to fail.

The discharge summary contains the feet, but does not obey them. It records corns, calluses, pes planus, hammertoes, pain, abnormal gait, and a movement resembling foot drop but expressly not foot drop. It even records a posture suggestive of dystonia. But the feet do not become governing. They become background. They become conditional. They become something that “may” have been separate, perhaps peripheral, perhaps related to another cause, perhaps suitable for podiatry.

This is not resolution. It is displacement.

A lawful and safe diagnostic sequence would have required the institution to do one of three things. It could have integrated the feet into the neurological diagnosis: for example, by treating them as dystonic expression of the movement disorder. It could have falsified them as the governing signal: for example, by showing that the gait and pain were not driven by the central neurological process. Or it could have explicitly superseded them: for example, by explaining why another tested explanation fully accounted for the presentation.

Integrate Falsify Explicitly supersede

The discharge summary does none of those things with adequate force.

Instead, it creates a grammatical hierarchy. Parkinson’s Disease is declarative. The feet are conditional. The primary diagnosis is fixed. The initiating problem is left uncertain. The abstraction receives final language. The body receives speculative language.

That is the structural fracture.

The importance of the feet is not merely anatomical. It is evidential. The feet are the continuity marker across the record. They are present before admission. They are present at referral. They are present during examination. They are present in the gait description. They are present in the patient’s protest. They are present in the later history. They are the site where the body kept saying that the problem had not been understood.

If a diagnostic frame cannot explain the most persistent and disabling signal, the frame may still be useful, but it is not complete. That is the difference between a working diagnosis and finality. A working diagnosis can coexist with unresolved facts. Finality cannot, at least not without clear qualification.

The podiatry referral is therefore not harmless in context. Podiatry may have been appropriate as supportive care. No one needs to argue that corns, calluses, hammertoes, or pes planus should be ignored. A patient with painful feet may need podiatric treatment. The problem is not that podiatry was mentioned. The problem is that podiatry appears to function as an exit route for the unresolved signal.

There is a crucial difference between support and displacement.

Support

This patient has a neurological disorder and also needs foot care.

Displacement

The foot-centred problem can be moved outside the neurological inquiry without explaining its neurological significance.

The discharge summary is vulnerable because it does not adequately mark that distinction. It does not say: podiatry is for surface foot care only; the dystonic-looking gait remains a neurological question. It does not say: the corns and calluses may be secondary to abnormal posture and require movement-disorder review. It does not say: the patient’s foot pain remains unexplained. It does not say: foot dystonia has been considered and requires specialist assessment. It simply allows the feet to drift outward.

That drift matters because the patient’s own objection was directed precisely at this point. Before discharge, he protested that the feet had not been addressed. That protest, if accurately recalled, was not an expression of general dissatisfaction. It was the patient reasserting the original scope of the referral. He was saying, in ordinary language: the thing that brought me here has not been answered.

He did not need to possess technical vocabulary to be right. He did not need to say “dystonia,” “diagnostic discriminator,” or “administrative foreclosure.” A patient can identify that a central problem remains unresolved without knowing the professional category into which it should fall. The body can be accurate before language catches up.

That is what happened here. The patient’s body had been speaking through the feet for years. The referral translated that signal into a neurological request. The discharge summary absorbed the signal into a Parkinson’s frame without resolving it. The patient protested. The protest did not enter the closure record. The feet were referred outward.

That is the sequence.

It is not enough for the institution to say that Parkinson’s disease can affect gait. Of course it can. The question is whether this patient’s specific foot-centred gait failure was explained. Nor is it enough to say that the patient had foot problems requiring podiatry. Of course he did. The question is whether those foot problems were cause, consequence, manifestation, or misdirection. Without that reasoning, podiatry is not an answer. It is a diversion.

The footdrop-like description is especially important. The record says the gait looked like foot drop but was not foot drop. That should have forced thought. If it is not true foot drop, what is it? If it looks like foot drop but is not peripheral weakness, why is it happening? The document itself gestures toward dystonic posture. That gesture should have pulled the feet back into neurology, not pushed them out toward podiatry.

Dystonia is not a decorative word in this case. A dystonic foot posture can produce abnormal loading, pain, callus formation, gait distortion, and apparent structural foot problems. If that possibility exists, then surface pathology cannot safely be treated as the explanation. The corns and calluses may be downstream evidence of abnormal central motor control. The foot may be the page on which the movement disorder writes itself.

That is why the feet were the signal.

The failure was not merely that the feet hurt. The failure was that the feet carried diagnostic meaning and the record did not let them govern.

This is also why later developments matter without being necessary. Later therapeutic response and genetic clarification may help explain what the feet meant. But the duty to take the feet seriously existed in 2017. It came from the referral, the examination, the gait description, the pain, the dystonic suggestion, and the patient’s protest. The later record confirms the importance of the signal; it does not create it.

The clean expert question is simple:

Where, before discharge and certification, did the institution resolve the foot-centred problem it accepted at referral?

If there is no answer, the diagnostic sequence did not complete.

That is why the feet cannot be treated as incidental. They are not merely part of the patient’s history. They define the accepted scope of inquiry. They test the adequacy of the discharge summary. They expose the weakness of the podiatry referral. They make the omission of protest material. They reveal the danger of finality.

The case entered through the feet.

The record closed over them.

That is the breach.
