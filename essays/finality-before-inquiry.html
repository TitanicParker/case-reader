Essay 10

Finality Before Inquiry

The master jurisprudential essay: the case as an ordering violation, not a hindsight complaint.

Governing rule Finality must follow inquiry. It must not precede it.

The cleanest way to understand this case is not as a dispute about diagnosis. It is a dispute about order.

Finality must follow inquiry. It must not precede it.

That is the governing rule. It is simple, but it carries the entire case. A medical system can be uncertain. It can act provisionally. It can make a working diagnosis. It can choose caution. It can defer treatment. It can order further tests. It can acknowledge that time and evolution may clarify what is not yet clear. All of that is legitimate.

What it cannot safely do is behave as though the question is settled while the process required to settle it remains incomplete.

That is what happened here.

The patient did not enter hospital as a clean Parkinson’s case. He entered through a foot-centred gait failure with neurological signs. The GP referral preserved uncertainty. It identified abnormal gait, stiffness, increased tone, brisk reflexes, severe foot pathology, deteriorating function, and the possibility of Parkinsonism or another neurological disorder. The hospital accepted that problem. It accepted a duty to explain the relationship between the gait, the feet, and the neurological signs.

That inquiry had to come first.

Finality had to wait.

The discharge summary shows that finality did not wait. It declared Parkinson’s Disease while the record still contained unresolved features that should have kept the case open. The patient was young. The presentation was atypical. The tremor was limited. The gait was abnormal and suggestive of dystonic posture. The feet remained central but unexplained. Brisk reflexes complicated the picture. Genetic or recessive disease was considered. Levodopa or related dopaminergic therapy was deliberately deferred. The clinician acknowledged that this may later be regretted. The clinician also stated that he was not a movement-disorders neurologist.

Those facts do not prove that the final diagnosis was impossible. They prove that the diagnostic sequence was incomplete.

That distinction is essential.

A working impression can coexist with incompleteness. A final diagnosis cannot, at least not without explicit qualification. If the record says “suspected young-onset Parkinsonism, atypical, pending further discrimination,” then uncertainty is preserved. If the record says “Primary Diagnosis: Parkinson’s Disease,” certifies that diagnosis, and moves the patient into administrative reliance, the uncertainty is no longer governing. It becomes background.

That is the failure.

The institution converted uncertainty into background and certainty into structure.

This is why the case should not be argued as hindsight. The later confirmation of PRKN mutations or dystonia may illuminate the record, but it does not create the original defect. The original defect is visible in 2017. The record itself shows that the case was not fully discriminated. The patient’s later genetic result is not the foundation of the argument. It is later confirmation that the earlier caution was not theoretical.

The real foundation is the order of acts.

  1. The patient entered through a foot-centred neurological problem.
  2. Parkinsonism was recognised.
  3. Atypicality was recorded.
  4. Levodopa was withheld.
  5. Possible regret was recorded.
  6. Non-specialist status was recorded.
  7. The feet remained unresolved.
  8. Parkinson’s Disease was finalised and certified.

That order is the case.

The most important legal and clinical question is not “could Parkinsonism have been suspected?” It could. The most important question is “what was the institution entitled to do with that suspicion?” Suspicion is not finality. Suspicion allows inquiry. Finality closes it. The record confuses the two.

This is particularly clear with levodopa. If levodopa had been started and produced a clear response, that response might have supplied important diagnostic information. If it had been started and failed, that too might have mattered. If it was deliberately withheld for therapeutic reasons, then diagnostic uncertainty remained. All three possibilities are medically intelligible. But only one is compatible with finality: the one in which diagnostic uncertainty has been adequately addressed by other means.

That did not happen.

The team chose not to use the discriminator, then allowed the record to behave as though the case had been discriminated. That is finality before inquiry.

The same structure appears with movement-disorder expertise. If the treating clinician was not a movement-disorders neurologist, that was not automatically a problem. It became a problem because the case was young, atypical, dystonic-looking, foot-centred, genetically possible, and levodopa-undiscriminated. In that setting, the disclaimer should have altered the pathway. It should have produced referral, supervision, or preserved provisionality. Instead, the disclaimer appeared inside the document that closed the case.

Again: finality before inquiry.

The feet show it most plainly. The feet were part of the presenting problem. The discharge summary did not fully explain them. It did not integrate them into a central movement-disorder mechanism. It did not falsify them as peripheral. It did not reason their displacement. It sent them outward. Podiatry may have been useful as supportive care, but it was not a substitute for neurological explanation. The initiating problem remained unresolved while the diagnostic label hardened.

Again: finality before inquiry.

The Long Term Illness certification is where the ordering violation becomes administrative. A diagnosis in a clinician’s working mind is one thing. A diagnosis certified for statutory purposes is another. Certification gives the label weight. It allows it to travel. It creates reliance. It makes later correction harder. If certification occurs while inquiry is incomplete, the system has not merely made a clinical judgment. It has created a durable administrative fact without entitlement.

That is why the LTI form is not peripheral. It is a hinge.

The DAT scan addendum then demonstrates confirmation after closure. The scan may have supported Parkinsonism. It may have reassured clinicians. But it came after the diagnostic frame had already been declared and certified. It was described as abnormal “as expected.” That language shows that the result was absorbed into an expectation already in place. It did not govern the original finality. It reinforced it.

Again: finality had already moved ahead of inquiry.

The omitted protest belongs in the same structure. The patient objected, in substance, that the feet had not been addressed. That was not a random complaint. It was a direct challenge to the completeness of the admission. If that protest was not recorded, the discharge summary lost the one real-time patient objection that exposed the ordering failure. The record closed more cleanly because the protest was absent.

That absence matters because the patient was cognitively preserved. He was capable of understanding. But he was not given the technical architecture needed to object in institutional language. He could only protest from the body: the feet remain unanswered. The system held the diagnostic vocabulary. The patient held the unresolved signal.

That is an asymmetry of knowledge.

It allowed closure to proceed without full confrontation.

This is why the later governance pattern is unsurprising. Once finality has attached prematurely, every later actor inherits a closed frame. The complaint becomes “clinical judgment.” The data accuracy issue becomes “disagreement.” The patient’s insistence becomes persistence rather than correction. The institution can keep returning to the settled record because the settled record is now the organising fact.

That is path dependence.

The system protects what it has already finalised.

This case is therefore not about demanding perfection from medicine. It is about demanding correct ordering from institutions. Doctors do not have to know everything at once. They do have to preserve uncertainty when uncertainty remains material. They do not have to treat immediately with every possible medication. They do have to recognise when withholding a treatment also withholds diagnostic knowledge. They do not have to solve a rare genetic condition in a week. They do have to avoid certifying ordinary finality when the record itself shows the case is not ordinary.

The rule is not harsh.

It is basic.

Permissible

Working diagnosis, provisional action, caution, further testing, specialist review, and explicit uncertainty.

Impermissible

Durable administrative finality while the initiating problem remains unresolved and material discriminators remain unused.

Do not close what you have not completed.

Do not certify what you have not sufficiently discriminated.

Do not turn a working hypothesis into durable administrative fact while the initiating problem remains unresolved.

Do not record red flags and then deny them governing force.

Do not disclaim expertise and then finalise as if expertise were complete.

Do not withhold the discriminator and preserve the certainty.

This is why “misdiagnosis” is too small a word. The issue is not merely that the final label may have been wrong or incomplete. The issue is that the system lost the right to treat the label as final when it did. The harm began not with a single erroneous word, but with a sequence inversion.

Inquiry was still open.

Finality arrived anyway.

That is the case.
