Essay 7

Confirmation After Closure

On the DAT scan addendum as retrospective reinforcement rather than governing inquiry.

Thesis The DAT scan was evidence. But in the sequence, it was evidence after closure.

Evidence note

This essay is an interpretive companion. It relies on the DAT scan addendum, discharge summary, LTI certification, and the chronology of diagnosis, certification, and later confirmation.

  1. Primary records pending upload
  2. DAT scan treated as post-closure reinforcement
  3. Chronology governs evidential weight

The DAT scan addendum is the kind of document that can easily be made to look decisive if it is read out of sequence.

It records an abnormal scan. It appears to support a Parkinsonian diagnosis. It can be presented as the objective result that validates the clinical impression. For anyone reading quickly, it may seem to settle the matter: the team suspected Parkinson’s disease, the DAT scan was abnormal, therefore the diagnosis was justified.

That is too simple.

The DAT scan must be read in time.

The question is not whether the scan was abnormal. The question is what role the scan played in the sequence. Did it govern an open diagnostic decision, or did it retrospectively reinforce a frame that had already become administratively final?

On this record, the second reading is the more important one.

By the time the DAT scan addendum arrived, the diagnostic and administrative machinery had already moved. Parkinson’s Disease had already been written as the primary diagnosis. The Long Term Illness certification had already identified Parkinson’s Disease as the condition for which the patient was under care. The patient had already been placed into a Parkinson’s pathway. The discharge architecture had already taken shape. The scan therefore did not operate as the point at which the institution decided whether finality was justified. It operated as confirmation after expectation.

That is why the language “as expected” matters.

“As expected” is not neutral language. It tells the reader that the scan was being interpreted through an existing diagnostic expectation. The result was not described as a fork in the road. It was not framed as a test whose result would determine whether the prior label should stand, be qualified, or be reconsidered. It was framed as confirmation of what the clinicians already thought.

That does not make the scan irrelevant. It does not mean the scan had no clinical value. It means the scan cannot bear the weight later placed on it. It may support the existence of a degenerative dopaminergic syndrome. It may support Parkinsonism. But it does not, by itself, resolve the entire diagnostic problem presented by this case.

The scan may support

Evidence of impaired dopaminergic transport or a Parkinsonian syndrome.

The scan does not answer

The unresolved foot-centred, dystonic, genetic, levodopa-response, and record-integrity questions.

A DAT scan does not answer the feet.

It does not explain the severe foot-centred pain.

It does not decide whether the foot posture was dystonic.

It does not distinguish every relevant young-onset or genetic Parkinsonian syndrome.

It does not resolve the significance of brisk reflexes.

It does not substitute for levodopa response where dopaminergic responsiveness is clinically material.

It does not transform a non-movement-disorder specialist into the appropriate final authority for an atypical young-onset case.

It does not document or answer the patient’s protest.

It does not make podiatry displacement reasoned.

Most importantly, it does not travel backwards in time and make earlier finality properly sequenced.

That is the central point.

A later supportive test can support a clinical suspicion. It cannot justify the premature conversion of that suspicion into administrative fact if the conversion happened before the test governed the decision. This is not a subtle legal trick. It is basic ordering. Evidence used after closure is not the same as evidence used before closure. Confirmation after the event is not the same as inquiry before finality.

The DAT scan therefore belongs in the case, but it must be placed correctly. It is not the foundation of diagnostic entitlement. It is a post-closure reinforcement. It is part of the way the frame stabilised after the frame had already landed.

That distinction matters because a defence may try to collapse the timeline. It may say: the scan was abnormal, therefore the diagnosis was reasonable. But reasonableness of suspicion is not the same as entitlement to finality. The scan may help explain why clinicians felt reassured. It does not answer whether they were entitled to certify and discharge as they did, when they did, on the record that existed before the addendum.

The scan also had limits within the differential. A positive or abnormal DAT scan is not a unique signature of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease. It supports impaired dopaminergic transport. It can be consistent with Parkinsonian syndromes more broadly. In a young, atypical, foot-centred case, that distinction matters. If the real unresolved question is not “is there dopaminergic deficit?” but “what kind of Parkinsonism is this, what is the role of dystonia, and how do the feet fit?”, then the DAT scan is not the final answer. It is one piece.

The discharge record itself shows that the question was broader than the scan. It raised possible genetic or recessive disease. It recorded atypical features. It recorded dystonic-looking gait. It recorded severe foot pathology. It recorded levodopa deferral. Those uncertainties were not all capable of being resolved by the DAT scan. So the scan could not safely be used to wash away the incompleteness that the same record had already preserved.

The phrase “Parkinson’s Disease or Lewy Body disease” in the report, followed by clinical exclusion of Lewy Body disease, also shows how quickly the result was narrowed into the pre-existing frame. The addendum does not open a fresh differential discussion. It does not say: given this abnormal scan, the case now requires movement-disorder classification. It does not say: the scan confirms dopaminergic deficit but does not resolve the atypical foot-centred features. It does not say: the primary diagnosis should remain provisional pending further review. It says, in effect: abnormal as expected; this is not Lewy Body disease; further chat to follow.

That is confirmation language, not open inquiry language.

Again, the problem is not that the clinicians took reassurance from the scan. The problem is that the scan is made to function as if it validates the sequence. It does not. It supports part of the clinical picture while leaving the ordering breach untouched.

The ordering breach is this: finality should follow inquiry. If the DAT scan was part of the inquiry, finality should have waited for it. If the scan was not necessary for finality, then it cannot later be used as though it justified finality. The institution cannot have it both ways. It cannot close before the scan and then treat the scan as the basis for closure.

This is especially important because the LTI certification had already hardened the diagnostic label. Once that occurred, the scan result entered a system already primed to receive it as confirmation. It was not being interpreted in an untouched diagnostic space. It was being absorbed into a frame that had already begun to produce reliance.

That is why “as expected” is so revealing. Expectation is the sign of prior commitment.

The expert question should be framed carefully:

Did the DAT scan addendum complete an open diagnostic process, or did it retrospectively stabilise a diagnosis that had already been declared and certified?

A second expert question should follow:

Even accepting that the DAT scan supported Parkinsonism, did it resolve the unresolved foot-centred and dystonic features sufficiently to justify the prior administrative finality?

Those questions prevent the scan from being treated as a magic key.

The scan was not nothing.

But it was not enough.

It was not enough because the case was not simply about whether there was dopaminergic deficit. It was about whether the institution was entitled to close a complex, atypical, foot-centred, genetically possible, levodopa-undiscriminated presentation as Parkinson’s Disease before resolving the initiating problem.

The DAT scan could support the suspicion.

It could not erase the incompleteness.

It could not answer the feet.

It could not supply the missing levodopa response.

It could not cure the absence of movement-disorder referral.

It could not restore the omitted protest.

It could not make statutory certification timely.

That is why this essay must be careful. It should not overstate. It should not deny the scan’s relevance. It should place it.

The DAT scan was evidence.

But in the sequence, it was evidence after closure.

And evidence after closure is not the same as inquiry before closure.
